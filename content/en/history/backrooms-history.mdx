Most internet horror burns out in a week. The backrooms did the opposite. A single blurry photograph of an empty yellow room grew into a sprawling shared universe with hundreds of levels, its own explorer groups, and a Hollywood adaptation. The backrooms history is really a story about how the internet builds mythology — and why liminal spaces unsettle us so deeply.

The short version: in May 2019, an anonymous 4chan user posted a photo of a vacant, mono-yellow office interior and joked that you get there by "noclipping" out of reality. That post became the seed of everything that followed. The rest of the backrooms history is a chain of fan wikis, found-footage films, and endless debate over what counts as canon.

## The Photograph Nobody Could Place

The backrooms history does not begin with a writer. It begins with an image that felt wrong in a way people struggled to articulate: a wide, empty room papered in mono-yellow, carpet that looked slightly damp, and a row of fluorescent lights humming at the ceiling.

According to widely cited accounts of the concept's origin, that photograph was taken in 2002 — years before anyone attached a story to it. Community reports later traced the image to a building that was being renovated at the time, which explains the emptiness, the exposed lighting, and the complete absence of furniture or people.

Why did that particular picture stick when thousands of other empty-room photos did not?

- **Familiarity:** it looks like an office, a hotel corridor, or a waiting room you have already sat in.
- **Emptiness:** no windows, no visible exits, no signage, no doors that lead anywhere obvious.
- **Wrong light:** the yellow cast reads as fluorescent decay rather than daylight.
- **Zero context:** no caption, no location, no photographer, no date attached at the time.

Every one of those details later became a rule in the fiction: endless, windowless, mono-yellow, and nearly impossible to leave.

## May 2019: The Post That Named the Fear

The concept itself was first posted on 4chan in May 2019 by an anonymous user, according to the most commonly cited origin accounts. The post paired the photograph with a short caption explaining that you "noclip" out of reality if you slip through the wrong part of the world.

"Noclip" is video game vocabulary — the glitch that lets a player pass through walls and into unfinished space. That borrowed term did more work than a thousand words of description would have. It gave readers an instant mental model: reality has a geometry, and sometimes it breaks.

The caption also gave the space a name. Two sentences, and the backrooms history had its founding document.

## How the Lore Grew: Levels, Entities, and Competing Canons

The earliest expansions were collaborative. Writers on creepypasta and Fandom wikis took the two-sentence premise and asked practical questions: What is down the next hallway? What lives there? How do you survive?

That is how the concept became **containment fiction** — a labyrinthine, seemingly endless maze of rooms and environments, as the Creepypasta Wiki describes it. It is also how the term "liminal space" migrated from academic writing into everyday internet slang, with the backrooms credited as the genre's most popular example.

Community reports describe the setting as a stack of numbered levels, each with its own rules. The most frequently referenced ones:

| Level | Nickname | Environment | Typical threat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level 0 | The Yellow Rooms | Mono-yellow wallpaper, damp carpet, buzzing fluorescents | Low, but easy to get permanently lost |
| Level 1 | The Habitable Zone | Concrete floors, exposed pipes, dim light | Moderate |
| Level 2 | Pipe Dreams | Narrow corridors lined with pipes | High |
| Level 37 | The Poolrooms | Endless tiled pools and warm water | Variable, often described as calm |
| Level ! | Run For Your Life | Hospital-like corridor | Extreme — standing still is fatal |

Entities filled in the rest. Most are community inventions rather than anything from the original post:

| Entity | Appearance | Behavior | Threat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smiler | Floating grin and eyes in darkness | Ambushes in unlit areas | High |
| Hound | Feral humanoid | Hunts in packs | High |
| Partygoer | Masked, cheerful, unsettling | "Recruits" victims into its group | Extreme |
| Faceling | Almost human | Mimics ordinary people | Unpredictable |
| Skin-Stealer | Wears a victim's appearance | Infiltrates explorer groups | Extreme |

Fan-made organizations followed, most notably the M.E.G. (Major Explorer Group), an in-universe research body that catalogues levels and issues survival guides. Items like almond water became shorthand for "safe to drink" — small pieces of shared vocabulary that let thousands of strangers write in the same world.

Because so many people contributed, the backrooms history splits into several branches rather than one storyline:

| Canon | Home base | Tone | Known for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wikidot-style canon | Dedicated wiki project | Structured, database-like | Numbered levels, explorer groups, survival guides |
| Fandom canon | Community wiki | Broader and looser | Accessible entity pages, easy entry for newcomers |
| Cinematic canon | YouTube and film | Grounded, found-footage | No wikis, no narrator, pure atmosphere |
| Archive-style projects | Independent writing collectives | Artistic, dreamlike | Atmospheric prose and retired or deleted levels |

None of these is officially "the" backrooms. They coexist, overlap, and contradict each other — which is part of the appeal.

## The Found-Footage Era and the A24 Effect

The next chapter of backrooms history belongs to video. In early 2022, a teenage creator named Kane Parsons — posting as Kane Pixels — released a found-footage short set in the yellow rooms. Instead of explaining the lore, it showed a camera crew wandering in real time, complete with shaky framing, dated video artifacts, and a growing sense that something is following them.

The short went viral, and a series of follow-ups built a self-contained narrative that ignored the wikis entirely. A feature-length adaptation with A24 followed, putting the concept on the same cultural shelf as other internet-native horror properties.

Why did the video format work so well? Because the original image was always a photograph. Found footage preserves that documentary quality — the sense that you are looking at something that was not meant to be seen.

## Why Liminal Spaces Still Haunt Us

There is a reason the backrooms feel less like a monster story and more like a mood. The MIT Press Reader frames the trend as the rise of an "institutional gothic," and traces the word *liminal* back over a century to anthropologist Arnold van Gennep, who used it for ritual threshold spaces — the in-between moments of a ceremony.

An office at night, a dead mall, an empty pool, a hotel corridor at 3 a.m.: these are threshold spaces. They are built for transition, not for staying. When you are stuck in one, the architecture itself becomes the threat.

That is why the setting supports so many interpretations. Some community members read it as cosmic horror. Others treat it as a comfort fantasy about escaping ordinary life. The Decaturian notes that backrooms levels range from comforting to genuinely disturbing, and that range is deliberate — it lets each reader pick their own version of dread.

## How to Explore the Backrooms History Yourself

If you are new to the lore, you do not need to read thousands of wiki pages. Start small and work outward.

- **Read the neutral overview first.** [Wikipedia's article on the Backrooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Backrooms) covers the origin and cultural impact without drowning you in fan canon.
- **Watch a found-footage series before reading wikis.** Visuals teach the atmosphere faster than text.
- **Pick one canon and stay in it for a while.** Mixing wikis, videos, and fan fiction too early gets confusing.
- **Check dates on community pages.** Older entries often contradict newer ones.
- **Treat everything as community reports, not facts.** No one owns the backrooms, and no wiki is authoritative.

| If you want… | Go to… | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A quick, factual primer | Wikipedia | Neutral summary of origin and reception |
| Deep level and entity detail | Community wikis | The densest lore archives |
| Atmosphere over exposition | Found-footage series | Best entry point for the mood |
| Debate and discovery | Fan forums and subreddits | Player experience and theory-crafting |
| Academic context | [MIT Press Reader on the institutional gothic](https://thereader.mitpress.mit.edu/backrooms-and-the-rise-of-the-institutional-gothic/) | Explains the liminal-space tradition |

One practical tip: keep a bookmark file. Any honest retelling of backrooms history has to admit that the canon shifts constantly. Levels get renamed, entities get retconned, and entire wiki branches get archived. Save what you like, because it may not be there in a year.

## FAQ

**When did the backrooms start?**
The concept was first posted on 4chan in May 2019, according to widely cited accounts. The underlying photograph, however, was reportedly taken in 2002, which is why the image feels older than the story attached to it.

**Who created the backrooms?**
The original post was anonymous, so no single author is credited. Everything after that first caption — the levels, entities, and explorer groups — was built collectively by thousands of internet writers and video creators.

**Are the backrooms real?**
No. The backrooms are a work of collaborative fiction. The original photograph shows a real empty room, but the endless maze, the entities, and the levels are entirely invented.

**Is there an official backrooms canon?**
There is not. The backrooms history has several parallel canons — wiki-based, cinematic, and archive-style — and none of them outranks the others. Pick the version you enjoy most.

The backrooms history keeps growing because it was never finished in the first place. It started as one anonymous sentence and became a place anyone can add a hallway to.

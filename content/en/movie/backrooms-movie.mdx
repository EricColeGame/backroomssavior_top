## Why the Backrooms Movie Became a Horror Phenomenon

In May 2026, a 20-year-old filmmaker who built his audience on YouTube turned a creepypasta about endless yellow rooms into the biggest opening weekend in A24's history. The Backrooms movie is not a licensed franchise sequel or a reboot of a familiar brand — it is an internet legend that grew up in public, one short video at a time, and then walked into multiplexes. That is exactly why the Backrooms movie matters: it marks the moment digital-native horror stopped being a niche curiosity and became a mainstream box-office force.

The premise is deceptively simple. "The Backrooms" began as online folklore — a single unsettling image of an empty carpeted room with dull yellow wallpaper and buzzing fluorescent lights, paired with the idea that slipping out of reality drops you into an infinite maze of similar spaces. Director Kane Parsons took that premise and built a web series around it on YouTube in January 2022, using 3D software to render rooms that felt wrong in ways viewers could not quite name.

That series caught Hollywood's attention. By February 2023, an adaptation was officially in development with Parsons directing, and A24 ultimately distributed the finished film. The result was a roughly $10 million production that outperformed every other title in the studio's catalog.

Why did it connect so hard? Joseph Bernstein of *The New York Times* tied the film to Generation Z's "deep unease with life lived online," drawing parallels between the labyrinth and pandemic lockdowns, recommendation algorithms, and generative AI. A story about being trapped inside a badly rendered copy of reality turned out to be the right story for the right decade.

## Backrooms Movie Plot Explained

The Backrooms movie opens with a prologue dated June 19, 1990. Naren Warne, a researcher with the Async Research Institute, is separated from his group inside the Backrooms and killed by an entity the camera never fully reveals. Async scientists later review the recovered footage — establishing that these spaces have been studied for decades.

The main story follows Clark, a failed architect running a struggling furniture store in San Jose, California, while battling alcoholism and a collapsing marriage. His therapist, Dr. Mary Kline, is working through trauma of her own. After Clark notices flickering lights and a glowing slit in a wall near the store's breaker box, he falls through it and lands in the Backrooms, escaping an unseen pursuer by a matter of seconds.

The film's second half shifts from survival horror into something stranger. Mary wakes tied to a chair in a room resembling a dining area, surrounded by three mostly motionless human simulacra that Clark claims are made of an edible white substance. He demands she continue their therapy sessions. Instead, she tells him the truth about his failures — and for a moment, it seems to work.

That moment is interrupted by a towering, distorted version of Clark dressed as the store's pirate mascot. The creature kills him and chases Mary through collapsing, surreal architecture until she reaches a flawed replica of the furniture store. She fights back with a chunk of concrete from her demolished childhood home, triggering an Async gas trap. Researchers in hazmat suits subdue the monster and take her into custody.

In the final stretch, Mary meets Phil, an Async scientist who explains that the company once manufactured MRI machines before the Backrooms became its primary research focus. Asked what she found inside, she repeats Clark's description: a faulty, misremembered copy of reality. The film ends inside the Backrooms, where a loosely copied interrogation room holds her own deformed simulacrum, silent and still.

## Behind the Scenes: How the Backrooms Movie Was Made

Parsons did not simply hand his concept to a visual effects house. He designed the liminal environments in Blender first, and production designer Danny Vermette adapted those digital models into physical construction. Vermette compared the process of fitting the sets together to a game of Tetris.

| Distributor | A24 |

| Filming window | July 7 – August 14, 2025 |
| Working title | *Effigy* |

More than 30,000 square feet of Backrooms sets were built across four sound stages. The production used roughly 37,000 square feet of wallpaper and 29,000 square feet of carpeting, much of it sourced locally in Canada. Verticality was deliberately emphasized so that actors had to physically negotiate the space rather than simply walk through it — and by multiple accounts, crew members genuinely got lost on set.

The design brief was an "architectural hell" that resembles a broken 3D modeling sandbox. Misaligned floors, intersecting arches, and objects clipping through walls create the sensation that reality itself has a rendering error. Parsons cited the *Portal* games, *Mr. Robot*, *Punishment Park*, *One Hour Photo*, and *Paranoia Agent* as influences, and the film maintains strict continuity with his original YouTube series.

The soundtrack leans into that uncanny atmosphere. Boards of Canada's "The Word Becomes Flesh" plays over the end credits, while "Ulterior Motives" — a former lostwave track — Payolas' "Eyes of a Stranger," and The Caretaker's "B1 – All that follows is true" all appear in the film.

## Box Office, Reviews, and Cultural Impact

The Backrooms movie opened far beyond expectations. Early projections suggested a $20 million debut weekend; by release week, estimates climbed to $40–50 million. The film ultimately grossed $38.4 million on its first day alone.

Its top international markets included China ($27.8 million), Mexico ($17.8 million), the United Kingdom ($17.6 million), Australia and New Zealand ($12.3 million), and France ($12.2 million). Parsons became the youngest filmmaker to reach number one at both the domestic and global box offices, and the film ranks as the second-highest-grossing horror release of 2026 and the fifth-highest-grossing independent film of all time.

The critical consensus praised the film as a startlingly assured debut that turns internet liminal spaces into something both mesmerizing and terrifying. Sonny Bunch of *The Bulwark* argued that while the movie can be overly explicit about its themes, its emotional honesty and visual creativity make it tremendously effective.

The industry reaction may prove just as influential. *The Hollywood Reporter* framed the film's success as evidence of YouTube-native filmmakers reshaping horror, and noted that studios are increasingly scouting Reddit and other online communities for intellectual property and new talent.

## FAQ

**Is the Backrooms movie based on a true story?**
No. The Backrooms movie is fictional, adapted from Kane Parsons's YouTube web series, which was itself inspired by the "Backrooms" creepypasta — an internet legend built around a single unsettling photograph.

**Do I need to watch the YouTube series before the film?**
You do not have to, but it helps. The film maintains strict continuity with Parsons's original videos, so viewers familiar with the series will catch references that newcomers might miss.

**How long is the Backrooms movie?**
The theatrical cut runs 110 minutes. The extended *Everything Must Go Edition* adds about 15 minutes of additional footage.

**Will there be a sequel?**
No sequel has been officially announced. Given the film's box-office performance, further installments in the Backrooms universe remain a real possibility, but nothing has been confirmed.

Searching for a **backrooms complete timeline** usually returns three conflicting answers, and that is not a bug — it is the whole point. The Backrooms grew from a single 2019 internet photo into a sprawling shared universe with rival wikis, a found-footage series, co-op video games, and a Hollywood adaptation, and every branch keeps its own calendar. A **backrooms complete timeline** therefore has to run on at least two tracks: what happened in the real world as the fandom grew, and what supposedly happened inside the yellow rooms. This guide lays out both, plus the found-footage continuity that most newcomers actually mean when they ask about the timeline.

## What "Backrooms Complete Timeline" Really Means

Ask ten fans for a complete timeline and you will get ten answers, because the community never agreed on one canon. Instead, three timelines run in parallel. The first is the real-world history of the idea — a single anonymous post that snowballed into a genre. The second is the collaborative wiki canon, where thousands of writers have catalogued levels, entities, and explorer groups. The third is the found-footage continuity created by Kane Parsons, better known online as Kane Pixels, whose YouTube series gave the Backrooms a cinematic backbone and a period setting.

None of these is more "correct" than the others, and that is worth stating plainly. Wiki lore is open-source worldbuilding that anyone can extend. The found-footage series is a single creator's story with a beginning, a middle, and an ending. Treating them as one timeline is where most confusion starts.

## The Real-World Timeline: 2019 to Today

The Backrooms began as a caption. In 2019, an anonymous post on 4chan's paranormal board paired a photo of an empty, yellow-wallpapered room with a warning about "noclipping out of reality" into an endless expanse of old, damp carpet. The image was ordinary, which is exactly why it worked — it looked like a place you might have already been.

Community researchers later traced the photograph to a renovation of a hobby store in Wisconsin in the early 2000s, a detail that fans often call the creepiest part of the whole story. Treat that origin as community reporting rather than confirmed fact; the trail is well documented but was reconstructed by hobbyists, not historians.

What followed was rapid, decentralized growth. A dedicated wiki appeared and quickly became the reference point for level numbering, entities, and explorer organizations. Video explainers, TikTok edits, and short horror films pushed the concept into the mainstream, and by the early 2020s the Backrooms had become one of the defining internet horror aesthetics.

| When | Milestone | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|
| 2019 | An anonymous post pairs a yellow-room photo with the "noclip" caption | Created the entire concept in one image |
| 2019–2020 | Community researchers trace the photo to a Wisconsin hobby-store renovation | Gave the image a mundane, unsettling real origin |
| 2019 onward | The Backrooms Wiki formalizes levels, entities, and groups like the M.E.G. | Turned a joke into a worldbuilding project |
| Early 2020s | Explainers, edits, and short films spread the concept | Made it mainstream |
| 2022 | Kane Parsons publishes his found-footage Backrooms video | Reset the visual language of the fandom |
| 2022 onward | Co-op games such as *Escape the Backrooms* and *Inside the Backrooms* launch | Made the lore playable |
| 2023 | A24 announces a feature adaptation with Parsons directing | Moved the Backrooms into mainstream film |

For the film side, you can follow announcements on [A24's official site](https://a24films.com/), and for the deepest lore archive, the community maintains the [Backrooms Wiki on Wikidot](https://backrooms-wiki.wikidot.com/).

## Inside the Lore: How Levels Work as a Timeline

Wiki canon does not use dates. It uses levels, and those levels function like chapters — each one marks a stage of descent, from the harmless-looking entry point to places no wanderer returns from. Newcomers often read level numbers as a strict sequence, but experienced writers treat them as a map with shortcuts, loops, and dead ends.

A few elements repeat across nearly every version of the story. Almond Water is the fandom's universal restorative, found in odd places and trusted by nearly everyone. Entities range from the predatory to the almost polite, with Hounds, Smilers, Partygoers, and Skin-Stealers among the most frequently cited in community reports. Explorer groups such as the Major Explorer Group (M.E.G.) and the Backrooms Nonaligned Trade Group exist to map, trade, and survive.

## Kane Pixels and the Found-Footage Timeline

If someone asks about the backrooms complete timeline and means a story rather than a wiki, they almost certainly mean Kane Pixels. The series presents itself as recovered footage: camcorders, VHS artifacts, timestamp overlays, and institutional documents from a research organization that investigated the phenomenon decades before the public knew anything about it.

Community reports describe the series as unfolding across four loose eras. The first covers the original incident, when an experiment or accident drops a person into the yellow rooms with a camera running. The second covers the institute era, told mostly through test footage, surveys, and internal memos. The third expands into larger expeditions that go badly, adding scale, casualties, and hints of other travelers. The final era resurfaces old tapes in a present-day frame, tying the 1990s material to a modern audience discovering it.

| Era | What happens | Lore it introduces |
|---|---|---|
| The first incident | A camcorder-era accident strands someone in the yellow rooms | The found-footage format and the core aesthetic |
| The institute era | A research organization documents tests and surveys | Institutional horror and document-driven storytelling |
| The expedition era | Larger teams enter, and events escalate | Entities, other travelers, and real stakes |
| The modern frame | Old tapes and reports resurface years later | Connects the period story to today |

The series is deliberately sparse. Entities are glimpsed rather than explained, and much of the horror comes from architecture that should not exist. That restraint is why it pairs so badly with wiki canon: one explains everything, the other explains almost nothing, and fans enjoy arguing about which approach is scarier.

## Comparing the Canons — and Building Your Own Timeline

If you want to write about, film, or run a game set in the Backrooms, the fastest way to sound credible is to pick one canon and commit to it. Mixing wiki levels with found-footage rules creates contradictions that readers notice immediately.

| Element | Wiki canon | Found-footage canon |
|---|---|---|
| Entry point | Level 0, "The Lobby" | A specific no-clip incident |
| Tone | Catalog and survival guide | Analog horror and cinema |
| Entities | Named species with behavior notes | Rare, ambiguous, often unseen |
| Groups | M.E.G., B.N.T.G., and others | One research organization |
| Timeline structure | Levels and numbered clusters | Decades and recovered tapes |
| Canon status | Open to thousands of editors | Controlled by one creator |

A few practical rules make any timeline project stronger:

- **Separate in-universe dates from publication dates.** A video uploaded in 2022 can be set in the 1990s, and readers will assume you confused the two if you do not say so.
- **Label your sources.** Mark wiki lore as wiki lore, and mark personal anecdotes as player experience.
- **Date your research.** Wiki pages change weekly, so note when you checked.
- **Anchor to verifiable events** — the 2019 post, the 2022 series, the 2023 film announcement — and keep speculation clearly labeled.
- **Do not invent numbers.** Specific dates, entity counts, and level totals are the fastest way to get corrected in the comments.

## FAQ

**Is there an official backrooms complete timeline?**
No. There is no single authority, because the Backrooms is a community-built universe with multiple competing canons. The closest thing to an official record is the wiki community's level archive, which is edited constantly and openly.

**What year do the Backrooms take place in?**
It depends entirely on which version you follow. Wiki canon is essentially timeless, with no fixed present day. The Kane Pixels found-footage series is set largely in the 1990s, framed by a modern-day rediscovery of old tapes.

**What is the final level of the Backrooms?**
Community consensus most often points to Level 9223372036854775807, commonly called "The End," but plenty of fans reject the idea of an ending at all. Treat any "final level" claim as fan theory rather than established fact.

**Where should a beginner start?**
Start with the Kane Pixels series for a coherent story, then browse the wiki for level and entity lore, and finish with a co-op horror game if you want to explore the space yourself. Once you have seen all three, the contradictions stop being confusing and start being fun.

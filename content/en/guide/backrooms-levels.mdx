The Backrooms is an endless maze of liminal spaces, and **backrooms levels** are the individual environments that make up its shifting landscape. If you've ever wondered why fans argue about Level 0 versus Level 37, or what that "[NO DATA]" tag means on a wiki entry, the answer comes down to one thing: cataloguing. Levels are the backbone of the entire mythos — the framework that turns one creepy photograph into a whole universe. Understanding **backrooms levels** is the fastest way to go from casual lurker to someone who actually knows their way around.

## What Are Backrooms Levels, Exactly?

In the fiction, normal levels form the structural skeleton of the Backrooms. They vary wildly in size, danger, and habitability. Most are described as effectively infinite, though "bounded" levels — spaces with real walls and real edges — do exist in the catalogue.

The defining trait is tone rather than geography. Levels are built to trigger uncanny familiarity: an office you've never worked in, a suburb you've never lived in, a hotel that feels wrong in a way you can't name. Physics and nature don't behave reliably the deeper you go, which is exactly why the documentation culture around these spaces is so obsessive.

Not every level is a death trap, either. Community reports describe stable, hospitable levels where groups have built bases, outposts, and even functioning settlements. Others are lethal enough that survival is measured in minutes.

### The anatomy of a level entry

Every well-documented level follows a similar template. Knowing the template makes the wiki far easier to read.

| Entry element | What it covers | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Level designation | The number, e.g., Level 9 or Level 9.2 | Tells you where it sits in the sequence |
| Environment description | Layout, look, sound, atmosphere | Your first clue about danger |
| Entities | Creatures or phenomena present | Determines whether you can linger |
| Bases, outposts, communities | Known inhabited areas | Potential allies and supplies |
| Entrances and exits | How you get in and out | The single most important section |
| Sub-sections | Contained areas within the level | Often hides the real hazards |

## How the Numbering System Works

The numbering isn't random, but it isn't tidy either. Whole numbers are primary levels. Decimals are sub-sections attached to a parent level. Everything else — the oddities, the gaps, the unfinished pages — has its own rules.

One thing newcomers notice immediately: the list jumps. You'll see 0 through 13, then a leap to 16, 17, 19, 20, and straight on to 37. Those gaps aren't errors. They reflect the fact that the catalogue is a living document assembled by many authors over many years, and not every slot has a finished page.

| Format | Example | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Whole number | Level 9 | A primary, standalone level |
| Decimal sub-level | Level 7.6 | A sub-section contained inside a parent level |
| Marked for rewrite | Level 4 ✱ | Currently being revised by the community |
| Open to rewrite | Level 6 ⬤ | Available for another author to rework |
| Insufficiently documented | Level 358 [NO DATA] | Not yet catalogued in usable detail |
| Unnumbered | Separate library category | Catalogued outside the numeric sequence |

Those little symbols and tags are community metadata, not in-universe facts. A ⬤ or ✱ flag tells you the page's editing status, and a "[NO DATA]" label is essentially an open call for eyewitness reports from anyone who has explored that level.

### Why gaps and revisions exist

The wiki's own documentation notes that levels are occasionally prone to instability, meaning any write-up is subject to change without warning. That's a clever in-fiction justification for an out-of-fiction reality: a collaborative project where pages get rewritten, trimmed, or archived as standards evolve.

Pages that fall out of favor don't always vanish. Some get moved to an archive hub for older or flawed entries, which is worth knowing if a level you remember reading about suddenly 404s.

## Sub-Sections: Levels Inside Levels

Sub-sections are the connective tissue of the Backrooms. They're locations within locations — sometimes a single room, sometimes a landmark, sometimes an area so large and different it barely resembles its parent.

The purpose is practical: a sub-section lets authors expand the lore of a main level without rewriting the original page. That keeps popular entries stable while still giving writers room to add new material.

| Sub-section | Parent | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Level 0.1 "Zenith Station" | Level 0 | Transit-hub atmosphere |
| Level 1.1 "Corrupted Corridor" | Level 1 | Flagged for rewrite |
| Level 6.1 "The Snackrooms" | Level 6 | A lighter, food-themed detour |
| Level 7.6 "Evacuation" | Level 7 | Emergency imagery |
| Level 8.1 "The Dead Caverns" | Level 8 | Darker take on the caves |
| Level 37.1 "The Sanguine Reservoir" | Level 37 | Bleaker than its parent |
| Level 700.1 "It sure is." | Level 700 | A punchline continuation |

Notice how many sub-sections invert their parent's tone. A serene level gets a grim annex; a dark level gets a comic one. That contrast is a deliberate storytelling device, and it's one of the reasons these entries get shared so widely.

## Survival Tips and Community Reporting

Everything below comes from player experience and community reports, not from any official rulebook. Treat it as folklore with a track record.

- **Read the exits first.** Entrances and exits are catalogued precisely because a fast retreat is often the only smart move.
- **Never assume physics holds.** Gravity, distance, and time are unreliable in the deeper levels.
- **Distrust stability.** Documentation changes without warning, so yesterday's safe route may not be safe today.
- **Flag [NO DATA] levels as unknown, not safe.** An undocumented level is not a harmless one.
- **Note your entry point.** If you can't say how you arrived, you can't say how to leave.
- **Travel in groups when possible.** Communities survive by pooling knowledge.

If you want to dig into the full catalogue yourself, the community-maintained [Backrooms Wiki levels list](https://backrooms-wiki.wikidot.com/normal-levels-i) is the most complete reference available, and it's updated constantly by contributors.

### Contributing to the catalogue

Adding a level isn't as simple as hitting "edit." Most wiki communities require a greenlight from reviewers before a new page goes live, and there's a guide hub explaining formatting, tone, and the expected level template. If you're writing your first entry, the two things reviewers care about most are originality of concept and consistency with established lore.

## FAQ

**How many backrooms levels are there?**
The numbering runs to at least 999, plus decimal sub-sections attached to many of those entries. In practice, far fewer are fully documented — a significant number carry a "[NO DATA]" tag, meaning they exist in the sequence but haven't been catalogued in usable detail yet.

**What does [NO DATA] mean on a backrooms level?**
It means the community hasn't gathered enough reliable information to write a proper entry. These slots are effectively open invitations for eyewitness documentation. Don't read them as "empty" or "safe" — read them as unknown.

**Which backrooms levels are the safest?**
Levels described as stable and hospitable, like Level 1, are the usual answer, and they're where most reported communities are based. That said, stability is always relative, and any level can become unstable without warning.

**Can anyone add a new level to the wiki?**
Generally yes, but not instantly. Most projects require you to submit an idea for approval through a greenlight process before publishing. Once approved, your page needs to follow the standard template so it fits alongside the existing backrooms levels.
